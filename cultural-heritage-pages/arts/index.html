FIELD NOTES / 01Clothing

Barong Tagalog

The art we wear.

✳Follow the embroidery across the chest: texture becomes the ornament.

Look closely: a barong is a canvas of embroidery. This sheer formal shirt brings together fine fibers, skilled needlework, and a distinctly Filipino sense of dress.

ROOTED IN
Tagalog region, Luzon
FIRST CHAPTER
Pre-colonial roots
MADE WITH
Piña, abacá & jusi
PART OF LIFE
Formal occasions; Barong Tagalog Week, June 5–11
01Origins & evolution+

The name comes from “baro ng Tagalog,” or clothing of the Tagalog. Rooted in the pre-colonial baro, the garment changed through the Spanish colonial period, gradually taking the form of the embroidered shirt we recognize today. Ramon Magsaysay’s choice to wear it at his 1953 presidential inauguration helped bring it to national prominence. In 1975, its recognition as national attire and the declaration of Barong Tagalog Week affirmed its place in Filipino public life.

02The craft & the details+

Traditionally made with fine fabrics such as piña (pineapple fiber), abacá, and jusi, the barong balances lightness with intricate embroidery. The surface is part of its story: a restrained silhouette gives the needlework room to speak.

03Meaning & living heritage+

The barong carries the history of a people adapting dress to their own climate, circumstances, and identity. Worn at ceremonies, weddings, and inaugurations, it continues to evolve through contemporary design while remaining a recognizable expression of Filipino heritage.

FIELD NOTES / 02Clothing

Baro’t Saya

History, in silhouette.

✳Compare the sleeves and layers in the three photographs; Filipiniana is a family of evolving forms.

Its name means “blouse and skirt.” From everyday clothing to elaborate Filipiniana, the baro’t saya tells a story of changing materials, silhouettes, and cultural encounters.

ROOTED IN
The Philippines
FIRST CHAPTER
Pre-colonial roots; colonial evolution
MADE WITH
Fine fabrics, embroidery & layered dress
PART OF LIFE
Cultural celebrations and August language festivities
01Origins & evolution+

Before Spanish colonization, women wore garments including the tapis, a cloth wrapped around the body. As clothing changed under Spanish influence, the baro and saya developed into forms using lighter, finer fabrics and longer skirts. Later styles included the Maria Clara, traje de mestiza, and the terno.

02The craft & the details+

A blouse, a skirt, layers of fabric, and carefully considered proportions turn clothing into a visual language. The later terno is especially recognizable for its butterfly sleeves. These related forms belong to the wider world of Filipiniana; they are connected, but each has its own silhouette and history.

03Meaning & living heritage+

Named for the character in José Rizal’s Noli Me Tangere, the Maria Clara reflects the interwoven histories of Spain and the Philippines. Today, designers continue to reinterpret Filipiniana, making heritage something that can be worn, adapted, and carried forward.

FIELD NOTES / 03Architecture

Bahay Kubo

Built to belong.

✳Trace the roofline down to the raised floor: the structure is an answer to its surroundings.

A house can be a work of design as well as a place to live. The bahay kubo brings local materials, tropical weather, and everyday family life into one thoughtful structure.

ROOTED IN
Philippine lowlands
FIRST CHAPTER
Pre-colonial
MADE WITH
Bamboo, wood & nipa
PART OF LIFE
Everyday living; the spirit of bayanihan
01Origins & evolution+

Found across the Philippine lowlands, the traditional nipa hut developed from pre-colonial ways of building with nearby materials. Early one-room homes changed as families and their needs grew. Its design later helped shape the bahay na bato.

02The craft & the details+

A steep thatched roof sheds rain, while a floor raised on posts allows air to circulate and helps protect the living space from flooding and animals. The area below can become storage or a workshop. Openings and lightweight materials help the house respond to a hot, humid climate.

03Meaning & living heritage+

The bahay kubo is closely associated with bayanihan: neighbors working together, traditionally even lifting a house to move it. It stands for communal care and a way of living with nature. Its use of local materials and natural ventilation continues to inspire conversations about sustainable design.

FIELD NOTES / 04Functional art

Kalesa

A slower kind of story.

✳Notice how the carriage’s practical shape becomes part of the streetscape around it.

Two wheels, a covered seat, and the rhythm of a horse: the kalesa brings craftsmanship into motion. Once an everyday vehicle, it remains closely connected to the streets of historic cities.

ROOTED IN
Introduced from Spain; Manila & Vigan
FIRST CHAPTER
18th century
MADE WITH
Carriage body, wheels & decorative detailing
PART OF LIFE
Viva Vigan Binatbatan Festival of the Arts, late April–early May
01Origins & evolution+

Introduced during Spanish colonization in the eighteenth century, the kalesa carried people through daily life and ceremonial occasions. Motorized transport gradually changed its role, especially after World War II. Today, carriages remain visible in heritage districts such as Intramuros and Vigan.

02The craft & the details+

The covered passenger seat and large wheels give the carriage its recognizable form. In Vigan’s annual kalesa design competition and parade, decoration transforms this practical vehicle into a showcase of local products, artistry, and cultural motifs.

03Meaning & living heritage+

A kalesa connects the present-day street to an older urban rhythm. Its continued presence in heritage districts preserves a visible part of local transport history, while festivals give makers opportunities to reinterpret a familiar form.

FIELD NOTES / 05Functional art

Jeepney

A canvas on the road.

✳Read the colors and lettering as well as the route: each vehicle has a visual identity.

The jeepney turns a shared journey into a visual experience. Lettering, color, and decoration give a practical vehicle a personality of its own.

ROOTED IN
The Philippines; makers in Las Piñas
FIRST CHAPTER
After World War II
MADE WITH
Repurposed vehicles, metalwork & painted detail
PART OF LIFE
Everyday life on Philippine streets
01Origins & evolution+

After World War II, Filipino mechanics adapted surplus American military jeeps to help restore public transport. In 1953, Leonardo Sarao, a former kalesa driver turned mechanic, established the workshop that became Sarao Motors. Local builders helped turn a practical response to a transport shortage into an enduring icon.

02The craft & the details+

A jeepney’s body is also a canvas. Bright paint, hand-lettered signs, and decorative details reflect the choices of builders and drivers. The work connects metal fabrication with local painting and design, bringing artistry into the daily commute.

03Meaning & living heritage+

Often called the “King of the Road,” the jeepney represents Filipino ingenuity and resilience. Its continuing evolution raises questions about transport, livelihoods, and cultural preservation: how can an everyday icon move forward while carrying the stories of the people who make and use it?
