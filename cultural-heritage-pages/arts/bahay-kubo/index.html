ARCHITECTURE · THE ART OF BELONGING

Bahay KuboA home in conversation with its surroundings.

A raised floor, an airy wall, a roof that makes room for shade. The bahay kubo brings artistic form and practical knowledge together, reminding us that architecture begins with how people live in a place.

Climate & everyday life

Built from a knowledge of place.

The bahay kubo is a Philippine vernacular dwelling associated with local plant materials, raised living spaces, and a high roof. Honey Ibanez’s architectural history study at Delft University of Technology examines its relationship with climate, community, and the changing idea of Philippine identity.

The study also considers how colonial-era transformations and later interpretations carried aspects of the dwelling into other architectural forms. The bahay kubo is one entry into Philippine architecture, not a model for every region: local materials, environments, and building traditions differ across the archipelago.

The most useful lesson is to ask how a building works. Where does air enter? What makes shade? How do the materials meet? These questions turn a familiar image into a way of thinking about architecture and the people who use it.

THE LANGUAGE OF MAKING

Beauty is
in the details.

A generous line of shelter

Trace the high roof and its overhang. Think about the relationship between shape, shade, and rain. A strong outline can be visually memorable because it answers an everyday need.

Space above and below

Find the posts and the floor they support. The relationship between ground, raised room, and space beneath the house is one of the clearest ways to read its construction.

A texture made of many parts

Look at the walls, floor, and structural pieces separately. Bamboo, wood, and thatch contribute different textures; their arrangement brings pattern into the building itself.
