01 — THE BELIEF

Pagpag

A pause between the wake and home.

After a wake or funeral, some mourners stop somewhere else before returning home: a small detour meant to leave the presence of the dead behind.

Place of origin

The Philippines; practiced nationwide and among Filipinos abroad.

Date of origin

No documented date. The belief is passed down orally.

When it is practiced

After attending a wake or funeral; there is no fixed season.

THE PRACTICE

Pagpag means shaking off dust or dirt. Rather than going straight home, mourners may stop at a convenience store, mall, park, or another public place. Some families also change clothes at home and avoid sleeping in what they wore to the wake.

THE MEANING

The spirit of the deceased is meant to remain with the dead. The detour can mark a boundary between the wake and the household, while giving mourners a familiar ritual for closing a difficult day.

CONTEXT & CARE

All Saints’ and All Souls’ Days, November 1–2, bring many Filipinos to cemeteries. That seasonal connection is context, not a fixed date for pagpag. Some accounts trace the belief to pre-Spanish spirit traditions; that origin is not firmly documented. The Catholic customs around death may also shape how families practice it, but that is an interpretation rather than a settled source claim.

WHY IT STILL MATTERS
  • The practice continues through family habit and respect, even for people who are unsure about its supernatural explanation.
  • A young practitioner described continuing pagpag out of habit and respect for tradition.
  • The film Pagpag: Siyam na Buhay uses the belief as horror fiction, imagining dire consequences when the ritual is ignored.
  • In 2022, the Archdiocese of San Fernando’s Office of Exorcism suggested a cleansing prayer as an alternative.
